# Chapter 3 — The Ecosystem Today: Good & Bad

**Target:** ~5 minutes

---

So where are we right now? The AT Protocol ecosystem in 2026 is vibrant, growing, and genuinely exciting. But it also has real problems. Let us look at both sides.

## The Good

**A thriving app ecosystem**

The Atmosphere now has hundreds of apps. The AT Store lists 277 curated apps across categories: social, developer tools, games, publishing, productivity, and more.

Bluesky is the biggest app, but it is far from the only one. Let me highlight some of the most interesting projects.

**mu.social and EuroSky**

EuroSky is the biggest European PDS provider. It is run by Stichting Modal, a non-profit foundation based in the Netherlands. They provide the infrastructure — your account, your data, your PDS. On top of that, they built mu.social, a social app that is essentially a fork of the Bluesky app. Because Bluesky is fully open source, the EuroSky team took the code, adapted it for their European audience, and built on top of it. That is something worth highlighting for developers in this room: the protocol and the apps are open, so anyone can fork and build their own version.

mu.social has become one of the most popular Atmosphere apps. If you are European and care about data sovereignty, EuroSky is where you want your account, and mu.social is where you can use it.

**The non-profit model matters**

EuroSky is part of a larger trend in the ecosystem. The AT Protocol itself is not a company — it is an open standard that is being handed to the IETF for standardisation. Bluesky is a public benefit company, but it is separate from the protocol.

There are good examples of this non-profit model working well elsewhere. Signal is fully non-profit — no shareholders, no investors, just a mission. Proton has a non-profit foundation behind its for-profit services. Even OpenAI, however debatable its current state, started with a non-profit structure.

The point is: an open source, non-profit, or foundation-backed project is not automatically better because it is European or worse because it is American. What matters is that the code is open, the data is portable, and no single entity controls the network.

**PopFeed: your media library, yours forever**

PopFeed is a social platform for tracking and reviewing movies, TV shows, books, games, and music. It connects to TMDB and other data sources so you can log what you watch, read, and play. Your reviews and lists are stored on the AT Protocol, which means they are yours — not locked inside PopFeed. If PopFeed shuts down or you want to switch to another app, your data comes with you.

**Sifa ID: the LinkedIn replacement**

Sifa ID is a professional identity platform on the AT Protocol. It pulls your activity from across the network — posts on Bluesky, code on Tangled, articles on Leaflet, photos on Flashes — into one portable profile. Your colleagues can endorse your skills, and each endorsement is signed by their real account. It also doubles as a personal website. People are already calling it the LinkedIn killer.

**Tangled: social coding for developers**

Tangled is a git collaboration platform built on the AT Protocol. Think GitHub, but your code and your activity live on your own PDS. Developers can star projects, fork repos, and collaborate while owning their data. Dan Abramov, the creator of Redux, uses Tangled. It is one of the most active development platforms in the Atmosphere.

**Innovation in every category**

There are apps for almost everything now. Margin lets you annotate any web page and save highlights to your PDS. npmx is a modern browser for the npm registry built on the protocol. pckt.blog is a distraction-free blogging platform. Flashes and Grain for photo sharing. Germ for end-to-end encrypted messaging. Games like Chaos Soccer and Witchsky. And new apps appear every week.

What ties them all together is a single login. You use the same @handle everywhere. Your data flows between apps. You do not start from scratch.

**European leadership**

Europe is playing a major role in the Atmosphere. EuroSky, mu.social, PDS MOOver, Sifa ID, Tangled, Margin, and many more apps are built and hosted in Europe, with European privacy standards. This matters because it shows that the AT Protocol is not just an American project. It is genuinely global, and European developers are leading the way on privacy, portability, and user rights.

## The Neutral

**Private posts are coming**

One thing the protocol cannot do yet is private posts. Everything on the AT Protocol is public by design. That is a feature for developers building on the firehose, but it is a limitation for users who want to share something privately.

AT Protocol Spaces is now in alpha. This will add private posts, group conversations, and membership-based communities to the protocol. Toni Schneider, Bluesky's CEO, said: "Once we enable private data, it is like 10 or 100 times the number of use cases we can serve."

If they figure this out, it changes everything. The protocol will support both public and private communication, all built on the same open infrastructure.

## The Bad

**Moderation is still hard**

The AT Protocol's moderation model is powerful — anyone can run a labeler, anyone can subscribe to the moderation they trust. But in practice, moderation is still fragmented and uneven. Bluesky handles moderation for its own app, but smaller apps and independent PDS providers have to figure it out themselves. The tools are getting better, but they are not there yet.

DDoS attacks have also been a problem. Tangled recently dealt with a "rather extensive DDoS attack" that took them offline. As the ecosystem grows, it attracts attention — including attacks from people who do not like the idea of an open social web.

**Monetisation does not exist yet**

This is the elephant in the room. The AT Protocol ecosystem has no real monetisation model yet. Bluesky itself does not run ads. There is no ad inventory, no monetisation layer, no way for creators or developers to make money on the protocol.

For developers building Atmosphere apps, this means you are building on hope. Some apps have subscription plans (pckt.blog charges $4.44/month), some accept donations, and some are funded by grants or venture capital. But there is no built-in way to earn revenue from the network itself.

This is both a problem and an opportunity. It means there is no platform tax. No one takes 30% of your revenue. But it also means the ecosystem needs to figure out how to sustain itself. Subscriptions, microtransactions, paid features, protocol-level tipping — these are all being explored, but nothing is standard yet.

**Discovery is still early**

With 277 apps, finding the right one is not always easy. The AT Store helps, but there is no app store review process, no quality control, and no curation. Some apps are polished, some are rough. Users have to do the work of figuring out which apps are good.

**W Social: a cautionary tale**

There is one project that deserves special mention. W Social launched at Davos in January 2026, backed by Swedish investors and former ministers. It positioned itself as the trustworthy European alternative to Twitter. It was built on the AT Protocol.

But then it deleted its public source code repository. No explanation. No deprecation. Just gone.

Despite this, EU institutions moved their official accounts to W Social — the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, the European Central Bank. Official EU data ended up on a private, for-profit, Swedish company's servers.

W Social is what happens when the establishment decides it wants its own internet: take open source tools, rebrand them, perform openness just long enough to land institutional credibility, then close the door. It is a reminder that "European" is not a synonym for "ethical." Compare this to EuroSky — a non-profit, transparent, open development project building the same infrastructure the right way.

## The big picture

The ecosystem is in an early but exciting phase. The infrastructure is solid — the protocol works, the apps are growing, the users are coming. But the layers on top — moderation, monetisation, discovery — are still being built.

That is normal. Every platform goes through this. The difference here is that no single company controls the outcome. The community decides. If you do not like how one app handles moderation, you switch to another. If you have an idea for a better monetisation model, you build it. The protocol does not stop you.
